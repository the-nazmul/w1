Targeted Killings: Who the Victims Were

During the Hasina years, police killed thousands of people in so-called “crossfire.” This page looks at the ones who were linked to a party or an armed group — and asks who the country still remembers.

By Nazmul Ahasan

Data: Netra News, Body Count (2023) · 2009–April 2022 · 256 records

This project uses data from Netra News and its Body Count report (2023). It looks only at targeted killings — planned killings by the police. It does not count deaths in street protests or in jail. The question is simple: of these victims, how many were said to belong to a political party or an armed group, and which ones?

Between 2009 and April 2022, under Sheikh Hasina’s government, Netra News counted about 2,600 deaths linked to the police. Around 2,000 of these were “crossfire” or other planned killings. Out of those, only about 260 people were tied to a party or an extremist group. The rest were mostly said to be about crime or drugs. This page is about that small political group.

The result is surprising. Most of these victims were not Islamists or jihadists. They were alleged leftist extremists — people said to belong to secret, armed communist groups like the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party. They are the biggest group by far. And this data stops in 2022, so it leaves out the killings of 2001–2006 under an earlier government — which would likely make the leftist share even bigger.

First, the groups

Who is who

These are the labels from the source, put into four groups. The difference matters: a secret armed group is not the same as a normal political party.

Alleged leftist extremists163

Secret, armed communist groups like the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party. This is not the normal, legal left. Parties like the Communist Party of Bangladesh (CPB) — which back then often sided with the Awami League — are very different from the secret groups counted here.

Alleged jihadists26

Armed Islamist militant groups: Jamaat-ul-Mujahideen Bangladesh (JMB), Hizb-ut Tahrir, Ansarullah Bangla Team, and HuJI-B.

Mainstream opposition67

The normal opposition parties: BNP and Jamaat-e-Islami. Jamaat is an Islamist party, but it belongs here as a normal party, not an armed group.

Not here: the mass Islamists0

“Islamists” in the everyday sense — big movements like Hefazat-e-Islam — are not in this list at all.

The source splits these into two: 189 were called “extremists” (the leftist and jihadist groups) and 67 were called “political opposition” (Jamaat and BNP). Of everyone called an “extremist” and killed, 86% were alleged leftists.

        Read it carefully

        What this shows — and what it does not

        It is easy to read this like a scoreboard. It is not one. Here is what these 256 records can and cannot tell us.

        What it shows

        • A share, inside a small group. Among the targeted-killing victims Netra News could link to a party or an armed group, which group they belonged to.
        • A one-sided picture. The leftist group is far bigger than the jihadists — and bigger than the whole opposition too. That is the opposite of what most people think.
        • A long habit. The killings went on for over ten years, by three forces — Police, RAB, and joint forces. Not one bad night.

        What it does not show

        • The full number. These are a small part of about 2,000 targeted killings. Most victims were about crime or drugs, not politics. This is not a count of all killings.
        • Proof of guilt — or even membership. “Identity” is just what the police and newspapers said. It was never tested in court, because the person was already dead.
        • The reason. A label does not prove the label is why the person was killed.
        • The full time. It leaves out the 2001–2006 killings under another government. Adding them would likely make the leftist share bigger.
        • Protest and jail deaths. Every case here is “crossfire.” Deaths in protests or jail are not shown — and those would likely hit the opposition harder.

        Why “targeted” killings matter most

        One thing works the other way. This data counts only targeted killings — “crossfire” and fake “gunfights.” It leaves out deaths in street protests and in jail. If those were added, the opposition — BNP and Jamaat, who were out on the streets — would probably be a much bigger share.

        But that is exactly why targeted killings matter on their own. A protest death can be chaos — a bullet in a crowd. A “crossfire” is planned: the person is picked up, taken away, and shot, with a fake story ready. Planned means the state meant to do it. So the fact that most of the planned killings hit alleged leftists is a strong, separate point.

        The point

        Everyone here was owed a trial

        Whatever these 163 people were said to be — party men, extortionists, members of a banned group — almost none of it was ever proven. A “crossfire” is not a court verdict. If the state had a case, the right thing was to arrest, charge, and try them — the same fair trial that people today rightly want for Jamaat leaders and other victims of the same years.

        A fair trial is not a prize for people we like.

        The right to a fair trial is for the accused exactly because nothing is proven yet. It does not depend on whether we like the person or share their politics. Take it away from an alleged communist, and you make it weaker for everyone.

        And yet the dead are not treated the same. Today, people in Bangladesh talk a lot about Islamist and Jamaat victims of the Hasina years — and they should. But the alleged leftist extremists, the biggest group here, get almost none of that attention. Their deaths were counted, saved in a file, and then forgotten. Counting them here does not say they were innocent. It says it was never the state’s job to decide that with a gun.
