Targeted state killings in Bangladesh during Hasina-era

During the Hasina years, Bangladesh’s security forces — mostly the police and RAB — killed thousands of people in various types of extrajudicial killings. This page looks at the ones who were linked to an ideological entity — either political or armed — and asks who the country still remembers.

By Nazmul Ahasan · 22 December 2025

This project uses data from Netra News and its Body Count report (2023). It looks only at targeted killings — planned killings by the security forces. It does not count those killed during street protests or in jail. The question is simple: of these victims, how many were said to belong to an ideology-driven organisation, and which ones?

Between 2009 and April 2022, under Sheikh Hasina’s government, Netra News counted about 2,600 deaths linked to security forces. Around 2,000 of these were “crossfire” or other apparent planned killings. Out of those, only about 270 people were tied to political or outlawed extremist groups. The rest were mostly said to be about crime or drugs.

This page is about that small ideological group.

The result is surprising. Most of these victims were not Islamists or jihadists. They were alleged leftist extremists — people said to belong to secret, armed communist groups like the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party. These far-left militant organisations, combined, represent the biggest group by far. And this data stops in 2022, so it leaves out the killings of 2001–2006 under an earlier government led by the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) — which would likely make the leftist share even bigger.

One thing works the other way. This data counts only targeted killings — “crossfire” and fake “gunfights.” It leaves out deaths in street protests and in jail. If those were added, the opposition — BNP and Jamaat, who were out on the streets — would probably be a much bigger share.

But that is precisely why targeted killings deserve to be examined separately. A protester may be killed without having been individually selected, but a “crossfire” is planned: the person is picked up, taken away, and shot, with a fake story ready. Planned means the state meant to do it. So the fact that most of the planned killings hit alleged far-left militants is a strong, separate point.

Who is who

These are the labels from the source, put into four groups. The difference matters: a secret armed group is not the same as a normal political party.

Alleged leftist extremists163

Secret, armed communist groups like the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party. This is not the normal, legal left. Parties like the Jatiya Samajtantrik Dal, Ganosamhati Andolon or Communist Party of Bangladesh are very different from the secret groups counted here.

Alleged jihadists26

Outlawed Islamist militant groups: Jamaat-ul-Mujahideen Bangladesh (JMB), Hizb-ut Tahrir, Ansarullah Bangla Team, and HuJI-B.

Mainstream opposition67

The normal opposition parties: BNP and Jamaat-e-Islami. Jamaat is an Islamist party, but it belongs here as a normal party, not an armed group.

CHT regional groups13

The Chittagong Hill Tracts-based groups — the United People’s Democratic Front (UPDF) and the Parbatya Chattagram Jana Samhati Samiti (PCJSS/JSS) — are neither banned nor registered. They contest elections through independent candidates. Although they describe themselves as leftist, they are primarily identified as CHT-based regional groups and are therefore listed separately here.

        What the data does not show

        • These are a small part of about 2,000 targeted killings. Most victims were about crime or drugs, not politics. This is not a count of all killings.
        • “Identity” is just what the police and newspapers said. It was never tested in court, because the person was already dead.
        • A label does not prove the label is why the person was killed.
        • It leaves out the 2001–2006 killings under another government. Adding them would likely make the leftist share bigger.
        • Every case here is apparent planned killing or “crossfire.” Deaths in protests or jail are not shown — and those would likely hit the opposition harder.

        Everyone here was owed a trial

        Whatever these 163 people were accused of being — extortionists, members of banned groups or something else — almost none of it was ever proved. A “crossfire” is not a court verdict. If the state had evidence against them, it should have arrested, charged and tried them. That is the same fair trial people now rightly demand for Shibir activists and others persecuted during those years.

        That is what a fair trial is for: deciding whether an allegation is actually true. The right does not depend on whether we like the accused or agree with their politics. Deny it to an alleged communist, and you weaken it for everyone.

        But not all victims are remembered in the same way. Bangladesh now talks a great deal about Islamists killed during the Hasina years — and rightly so. Alleged leftist extremists, however, make up the largest group in this data and yet receive almost no attention.

        Including them does not mean declaring them innocent. Members of some of these groups were widely accused of murder, extortion and other serious crimes. But when alleged jihadists were killed, their supporters often argued — sometimes correctly — that they had been denied a fair trial, that some posed no immediate threat, or that innocent men had simply been branded as terrorists.

        Why was the same question almost never asked about alleged leftist extremists? Were some innocent people killed and then labelled members of outlawed groups, just as security forces had done with other supposed enemies? We do not know, because almost nobody seriously investigated it.

        In my research, I found no substantial investigation into this deadly chapter of state violence in Bangladesh. Even the few people who raised questions online were usually brushed aside, as though the victims automatically deserved what happened to them. That is all the more reason to be sceptical and try to establish the truth.

        There are also broader questions worth examining. Bangladesh’s far-left groups had documented links with similar organisations in India. So whereas the wider crackdown on Islamists is often seen as an attempt to appease India, why are the killings of alleged Naxalites in Bangladesh — whose counterparts are routinely killed by Indian security forces — not viewed through the same lens?

        Some have connected the killing of Sarbaharas to the later rise of jihadist groups such as the JMB.

        The aggressive campaigns carried out by the Western-backed Rapid Action Battalion also raise an uncomfortable question: did the West’s long-standing focus on anti-communism shape, directly or indirectly, the decisions that were made? If the US-led War on Terror can be blamed for the assault on Islamism in Bangladesh, why do we forget the US’ equally overzealous history of anti-communism?

        These are difficult questions, but that is precisely why they need to be investigated. The public interest in uncovering the truth could hardly be greater.
