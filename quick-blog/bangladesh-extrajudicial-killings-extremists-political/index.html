Targeted Killings — Identity Breakdown

Of the people killed in so-called “crossfire” during the Hasina years, who was tied to a political party or an alleged extremist group — and who has the country actually remembered?

Data: Netra News, Body Count (2023) · 2009–April 2022 · 256 records

This mini-project draws on the dataset behind Netra News’s Body Count investigation (2023). It looks only at people killed in targeted, extrajudicial executions — distinct from protest-related shootings or deaths in custody — and asks a narrow question: of those victims, how many were identified as having a political affiliation or an alleged link to an extremist group, and which groups?

From 2009 through April 2022, under Sheikh Hasina’s government, Netra News catalogued roughly 2,600 deaths involving law enforcement across three categories: targeted killings, lethal force during protests, and custodial torture. About 2,000 were classified as “crossfire” or other premeditated, targeted killings. Within that subset, only around 260 victims could be tied to a political party or a political or religious extremist group — the rest were largely framed as crime- or drug-related. This page is about that political and extremist sliver.

The pattern it reveals runs against the popular assumption. These alleged leftist extremists — reportedly tied to clandestine, armed outfits such as the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party, and a world apart from the mainstream, legal left — are the single largest group by far: they outnumber the alleged jihadists (JMB and similar) many times over, and outnumber even the entire registered opposition, BNP and Jamaat-e-Islami, counted together. Because the data stops at Hasina’s tenure, it also omits the extrajudicial killings of 2001–2006 under a previous government, which would likely tilt the balance further toward the left.

Before the charts

How these groups are defined

The labels below are the source’s, tidied into four buckets. The distinctions matter — they are the difference between a banned armed cell and a registered political party.

Alleged leftist extremists163

Underground, typically armed communist outfits — the Purba Bangla Sarbahara Party and its offshoots. This is not the mainstream, legal left: parties such as the Communist Party of Bangladesh (CPB), which in this period were often aligned with the Awami League’s ideological ends, are a different world from the clandestine cells counted here.

Alleged jihadists26

Armed Islamist militant groups: Jamaat-ul-Mujahideen Bangladesh (JMB), Hizb-ut Tahrir, Ansarullah Bangla Team, and Harkatul Jihad-al Islami Bangladesh (HuJI-B).

Mainstream opposition67

The registered opposition parties: BNP and Jamaat-e-Islami. Jamaat is an Islamist party, but it belongs here as street-level political opposition — not among the armed underground.

Not present here: the mass Islamists0

“Islamists” in the everyday Bangladeshi sense — mass, movement groups such as Hefazat-e-Islam — do not appear in this targeted-killing subset at all.

The source draws its own binary line through these four buckets: 189 of the dead were cast as “extremists” (the leftist and jihadist rows) and 67 as “political opposition” (Jamaat and BNP). Of everyone branded an extremist and shot in “crossfire,” 86% were alleged leftists.

        Read it carefully

        What this data does — and does not — show

        A chart like this is easy to misread as a scoreboard. It is not one. Here is what these 256 records can and cannot support.

        What it shows

        • A distribution, within a subset. Among the targeted-killing victims of 2009–April 2022 whom Netra News could tie to a party or an alleged extremist group, how those affiliations break down.
        • A lopsided one. People labelled leftist-extremist outnumber the alleged jihadists many times over, and outnumber the whole registered opposition too — the opposite of the common assumption.
        • A sustained practice. The killings recur across more than a decade and three arms of law enforcement — Police, RAB, and joint forces — not a single rogue episode.

        What it does not show

        • The full toll. These are a labelled slice of ~2,000 targeted killings; most victims were tied to crime or drugs, not politics. This is not a count of all extrajudicial killings.
        • Proven guilt — or even membership. “Identity” is how each person was characterised in police accounts and press reports: alleged, not adjudicated. No court ever tested the label, because the person was already dead. In the source, 189 were cast as “extremists” and 67 as “political opposition” — a line drawn by the state and the press, not a trial.
        • Cause. A label is not evidence that the label is why someone was killed.
        • The whole timeline. It omits the 2001–2006 killings under a different government; adding them would likely widen the leftist share.
        • Protest and custodial deaths. Every record here is the official “crossfire” script; deaths in street protests or in custody are separate categories, not shown — and those would likely fall more heavily on the political opposition.

        Why “targeted” is the sharper test

        One caveat cuts the other way. This dataset counts only targeted killings — “crossfire” and staged “gunfights.” It leaves out deaths during street protests and in custody. Had those been included, the political opposition — BNP and Jamaat, whose activists filled the streets — would almost certainly make up a much larger share, because that is who was out there to be shot at.

        But that is exactly why the targeted killings deserve their own reckoning. A protest death can be chaos — a bullet in a crowd, a panicked line of police. A “crossfire” is planned: a person is picked up, driven out, and shot, with the cover story written in advance. Targeted implies premeditation, and premeditation implies a higher order of state intent and complicity. That the deliberate, pre-planned killings fell most heavily on alleged leftists is not cancelled out by the protest numbers. It is a separate, and sharper, fact.

        The point

        Everyone here was owed a trial

        Whatever these 163 people were alleged to be — party cadres, extortionists, members of a banned underground outfit — almost none of it was ever proven. A “crossfire” is not a verdict; it is the absence of one. If the state believed it had a case, the lawful answer was arrest, charge, and trial: the very due process that Bangladeshis today rightly demand for Jamaat leaders and other victims of the same era.

        Due process is not a reward for the sympathetic.

        The right to a fair trial is owed to the suspect precisely because nothing has yet been proven. It does not depend on whether we like the accused, share their politics, or believe the charge. Strip it from the alleged communist and you have already loosened it for everyone else.

        And yet the dead have not been mourned on the same terms. The public conversation in Bangladesh has become intensely attentive to Islamist and Jamaat victims of the Hasina years — understandably so. The alleged leftist extremists — the largest single group in this dataset by a wide margin — have drawn little of that same attention. Their deaths were tallied, archived, and largely forgotten. Counting them here is not a claim that they were innocent. It is a claim that innocence was never the state’s to decide with a gun.
